#### A first guess at the right measure

If you knew nothing about the field and were asked to invent a measure of information, what might you try?

A first guess: count the number of possible messages. If there are MMM equally likely messages and you receive one, you have learned the answer to a question with MMM possibilities. The natural measure of "how big a question" is just MMM. This is the kind of move a beginner makes, and it is wrong, but instructively wrong.

To see why it is wrong, consider two independent events. A friend sends you the outcome of two independent coin flips. The first flip has 222 possible outcomes; the second also has 222. Together, they have 2脳2=42 \times 2 = 42脳2=4 possible joint outcomes. If your measure of information were just MMM, then the two-flip message would carry "four units" while one flip carries "two units." But intuitively, two independent flips carry exactly twice as much information as one flip, since they are independent. The right measure should _add_ under independence, not multiply.

The fix: take the logarithm. log鈦?M1鈰匨2)=log鈦1+log鈦2\log(M_1 \cdot M_2) = \log M_1 + \log M_2log(M1鈥嬧媴M2鈥?=logM1鈥?logM2鈥? Independence in joint distributions becomes addition in log space. Define the information in a message that picks one of MMM equally likely options to be log鈦\log MlogM, and additivity is automatic.

This was the proposal Ralph Hartley made in 1928, two decades before Shannon. Hartley's measure works for the easy case (equally likely outcomes) but fails for the typical case, in which different outcomes have different probabilities. If a friend tells you "it rained today" in Seattle, you learn very little; rain in Seattle is common. If a friend tells you "it rained today" in the Atacama desert, you learn a great deal; rain in the Atacama is exceedingly rare. Both messages are the same string, "it rained today," but they resolve different amounts of uncertainty.

Hartley's measure cannot distinguish them. We need one that can. Shannon's was the next move.


### 1.2 Self-Information

The right generalization is to attach an information value not to a _message_ but to an _outcome_. If outcome xxx has probability p(x)p(x)p(x), then receiving the news that xxx occurred resolves an amount of uncertainty that depends on p(x)p(x)p(x), specifically on how small p(x)p(x)p(x) is. The smaller p(x)p(x)p(x), the more surprising the message, and the more information it carries.

**Q3.** Consider a variant on the entropy axioms: drop Axiom 4 (normalization). What functions then satisfy the remaining three axioms? What does this tell you about the role of "choice of unit" in physics and information theory?

**Discussion:** Without normalization, the function H=鈭抍鈭憄ilog鈦iH = -c \sum p_i \log p_iH=鈭抍鈭憄i鈥媗ogpi鈥?for any c>0c > 0c>0 satisfies the remaining axioms. In other words, removing normalization is exactly removing the choice of unit (bits versus nats versus dits). Choice of unit is universal in physics: temperature can be measured in Kelvin, Celsius, Fahrenheit, Rankine; energy in joules, ergs, calories. Each unit corresponds to a multiplicative constant. The axiomatic uniqueness of entropy _up to a multiplicative constant_ is exactly the statement that information is a real physical quantity with a natural definition, and the only choice is what we measure it in.

This connects information theory to thermodynamics in an unexpected way. The thermodynamic entropy S=鈭択B鈭憄iln鈦iS = -k_B \sum p_i \ln p_iS=鈭択B鈥嬧垜pi鈥媗npi鈥?is the same formula with kBk_BkB鈥?(Boltzmann's constant) playing the role of unit. Many physicists and information theorists have argued, most famously E. T. Jaynes in 1957, that thermodynamic entropy is a special case of Shannon entropy, applied to the distribution over microstates. We will encounter this perspective again in Chapter 9 when we discuss the maximum-entropy principle in statistical mechanics.

### 3.9 Historical Note: McMillan, Breiman, and the Bell Labs Years

Shannon's 1948 proof of the AEP was the i.i.d. case, the easy half. Real sources are not i.i.d., and Shannon knew it: his 1951 paper on the entropy of English explicitly used n-gram statistics for n up to 8. But proving the AEP for general stationary ergodic sources required machinery he did not have on hand.

Brockway McMillan, a younger Bell Labs colleague, supplied the first half of the generalization in 1953, extending the AEP to stationary ergodic processes via direct calculation. Leo Breiman, then a Berkeley statistician, sharpened McMillan's result in 1957 from convergence in probability to almost-sure convergence using the Birkhoff ergodic theorem. The combined result is what we now call the _Shannon鈥揗cMillan鈥揃reiman theorem_.

Bell Labs in the 1950s was an unusual place. Shannon worked alongside John Tukey (who would later coin "bit" and "software"), Richard Hamming (Chapter 7), David Slepian (Chapter 12), and Claude Berrou's intellectual ancestors. The labs paid for fundamental research with no immediate application, and the AEP, useless for any product the company was making in 1953, was nevertheless taken seriously enough that McMillan got six months of free time to work on it. The institutional patience that produced these foundational results is largely gone from corporate research; almost all subsequent extensions of the AEP have come from academic settings.


### Common Misconception

Two stumbles are common in interpreting the AEP.

* **Misconception** : "Typical sequences are the most probable sequences."

No. The single most probable sequence of nnn i.i.d. flips of a coin with p(heads)=0.7p(\text{heads}) = 0.7p(heads)=0.7 is "all heads," with probability 0.7n0.7^n0.7n. Typical sequences are not maximally probable; they are _close to the average log-probability_. The most probable single sequence may be far from typical.

#### Limitations of Huffman

### Common Misconception

* **Misconception** : "Channel capacity is the maximum _transmission rate_ of any specific scheme."

Capacity is the maximum _achievable rate_ , where "achievable" means there exists a scheme (perhaps very complex) that uses the channel nnn times to transmit 2nR2^{nR}2nR messages with vanishing error probability as n鈫掆垶n \to \inftyn鈫掆垶. For any fixed nnn and any specific code, the achievable rate is generally less than CCC. Capacity is the asymptotic limit.

* **Misconception** : "The capacity of a noisy channel is zero: you can't reliably transmit through noise."

This is exactly the claim Shannon refuted. A noisy channel has positive capacity for any noise level less than maximal. By using long enough block codes, you can drive the error rate to zero while transmitting at any rate below capacity. This was the surprising and revolutionary content of Shannon's 1948 paper.

* **Misconception** : "Capacity tells you how to build a code."

Capacity is an existence statement. It says a good code exists; it does not say what the code is. Constructing capacity-approaching codes (Hamming, Reed-Solomon, LDPC, Turbo, Polar) is the subject of Chapter 7 and is, historically, an active engineering research program that took fifty years after Shannon's theorem to come close to capacity in practice.

#### Random initializations in deep learning

Less directly: deep learning relies on random initializations of network weights. The success of training depends on the initialization landing in a "good" region of weight space. While this is not literally Shannon's argument, the spirit 鈥?that random samples from a high-dimensional distribution tend to have desirable properties 鈥?is the same.

These are all instances of the same general principle: **in high-dimensional spaces, random objects tend to have surprisingly good properties**. Shannon's coding theorem was the first dramatic instance, but the phenomenon recurs throughout modern computer science.


### Common Misconception

* **Misconception** : "The channel coding theorem says you can transmit error-free at rate CCC."

No. The theorem says you can transmit at rates _below_ CCC with error tending to zero. At exactly CCC, the situation is more subtle: error may or may not tend to zero, depending on the channel. Above CCC, error is bounded away from zero.

* **Misconception** : "Shannon told us how to build good codes."

No. Shannon's proof is non-constructive: it shows good codes exist but does not specify them. The 50-year engineering effort to find capacity-approaching codes is precisely because Shannon's theorem was an existence result.

* **Misconception** : "The random-coding argument says random codes are optimal."

No. Random codes are merely _good enough_ : the average random code achieves capacity, so at least one specific code does. Specific structured codes (Reed-Solomon, LDPC, Polar) often perform better than a typical random code, while being decodable with practical algorithms.


### Chapter Summary

Shannon's Channel Coding Theorem is the deepest result in classical information theory:

  1. **Achievability** : Any rate R<CR < CR<C is achievable with vanishing error. Proof: random coding + joint typicality decoding.
  2. **Converse** : Any rate R>CR > CR>C cannot achieve vanishing error. Proof: Fano's inequality + data-processing inequality.

The capacity C=max鈦(x)I(X;Y)C = \max_{p(x)} I(X; Y)C=maxp(x)鈥婭(X;Y) is the _sharp_ boundary between achievable and unachievable rates. There is no continuous trade-off between rate and error; below capacity, you can have both.

The proof technique of random coding was Shannon's most influential methodological contribution. It shows that the average over all codes is good enough; therefore at least one specific code is good. Engineers spent fifty years catching up to construct codes that approach capacity in practice.

The next chapter treats those practical codes: Hamming, Reed-Solomon, LDPC, Turbo, Polar. The chapter after that turns to KL divergence, the central divergence of information theory and modern machine learning.
