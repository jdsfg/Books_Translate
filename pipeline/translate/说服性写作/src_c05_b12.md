#### Think Deeper

**Q1.** The chapter argues that the supplement is often the deciding factor between two comparable applicants. Yet many applicants treat the supplements as last-minute work. Explain the gap. Why do candidates underweight a form that admissions readers weight so heavily?

**Discussion:** Several explanations are partly true. The personal essay is more emotionally salient — it feels like the _real_ essay, while supplements feel like paperwork. Candidates overestimate the marginal effect of the personal essay and underestimate the supplements'. By the eighth or twelfth supplement, cognitive bandwidth has run out. High school counselors often emphasize the personal essay disproportionately, reinforcing the misallocation. The chapter's claim is empirical: at the margin, in committee, the supplements decide. The candidate who allocates effort to match the deciding factor outperforms peers who allocate by emotional salience.

**Q2.** Recycling supplements is officially discouraged. In practice, almost all candidates do some recycling. Where is the line between _reasonable recycling_ and _paste-and-swap betrayal_?

**Discussion:** The line is roughly: reuse content about _yourself_ that does not change between schools (a vignette about Saturday tutoring, a paragraph about your intellectual orientation). Do not reuse content about the _school_ that should change between schools (specific courses, faculty members, programs). The deeper test: if you remove school names from the recycled portion, does it still read coherently? If yes, it is reasonable recycling. If the recycled portion contains course codes or faculty names that have to be swapped, it is paste-and-swap and likely to be detected. The book's stronger claim: the school-specific paragraph should be written from scratch each time, no exceptions.

**Q3.** The "why this major" supplement is a curiosity test. Some majors are easier to demonstrate curiosity for than others. A candidate applying as a CS major can point to coding projects; a candidate applying as a history major has fewer obvious props. How should the candidate for a less-prop-heavy major demonstrate curiosity?

**Discussion:** Reading is the universal prop. A candidate who has read three books about a historical period and can articulate what each one missed has demonstrated curiosity. So has one who has visited an archive, read a primary source in translation, written an unassigned research paper, or attended a free public lecture. Every academic discipline has _some_ artifact a curious 17-year-old can produce. The candidate who has not produced one is not yet ready to claim the major; the candidate who has should describe it specifically. The "why this major" supplement is partly a filter — it surfaces who has done their own work and who has only filled out forms.
