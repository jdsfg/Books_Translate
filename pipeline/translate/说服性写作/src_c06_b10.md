#### Concept Check

**Q1.** What is the central reorientation between the undergraduate essay and the graduate statement?

**Answer:** The reader's question shifts from _who is this person?_ to _what research direction is this person prepared to pursue?_ Graduate readers read for evidence of a research orientation; childhood anchors and emotional vulnerability are out of place. The graduate statement is a research document with the candidate behind it, not a personal narrative with intellectual gestures.

**Q2.** Why is the statement _I am interested in cognitive neuroscience_ insufficient as a graduate research interest?

**Answer:** It is a topic, not a question. Topics are too broad to indicate readiness for graduate-level research. A question is more specific (e.g., _I want to understand whether the bilingual advantage in attentional control is robust across socioeconomic strata_). The question may turn out to be wrong or may be revised; what matters is that the candidate can articulate one.

**Q3.** What four questions should each piece of evidence in a graduate statement answer?

**Answer:** (1) What was the question being investigated? (2) What did the candidate specifically do? (3) What was the result, and what did the candidate learn (including from what did not work)? (4) How does this lead to the candidate's current research interest?

**Q4.** Why is the over-confident closing a pitfall in graduate statements?

**Answer:** Graduate committees know that two-year and six-year research predictions are rarely accurate. A candidate who claims certainty about exactly what they will work on for years signals naivety about how research evolves. The Restrained Connection from Chapter 2 applies: state plans concretely while acknowledging the specific question will evolve.
